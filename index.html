Methods in short

Source and scope. This Explorer analyses 49 research abstracts from the MLW Annual Scientific Meeting held in Mangochi, 4–7 October 2026. The original handbook provides the abstract titles, authors, text and thematic sections. The analyses describe this conference collection, not necessarily the full MLW research portfolio.

Research Streams. The section chart counts abstracts under the handbook’s original thematic headings. The five broader research streams and the assessment of cross-cutting strengths are interpretative syntheses of the abstracts. These streams may overlap, and the illustrative abstracts are examples rather than an exhaustive or exclusive classification. They do not measure funding, staffing or research impact.

Abstracts. The Abstracts view presents the source texts and allows searching and filtering. Its Level 1 filter assigns each abstract a predominant broad terminology domain based on the extracted terms associated with it; an abstract can contain terms from other domains. Its Level 2 filter uses the handbook’s original research sections.

Networks. The network links handbook abstracts to terminology domains, with an optional intermediate layer showing extracted technical terms. These links represent associations in this collection, not proven scientific collaborations or causal relationships.

Terminology. GPT-assisted analysis identifies technical terms, assigns terminology domains, generates explanatory definitions and estimates whether terms may be familiar across different disciplines. A technical term is a word or multi-word expression whose interpretation depends substantially on specialised disciplinary, methodological, scientific or professional knowledge. Extraction does not follow a fully specified, reproducible rule set or a verified controlled lexicon; it may reflect contextual usage and semantic specificity, the degree to which an expression conveys a specialised meaning in a domain. This differs from linguistic specificity, which concerns reference to a particular identifiable entity.

Expertise & Datasets. This dashboard brings together skills reflected in the abstracts, potential contributions from the Data Science and Computational Modelling Unit, and opportunities to use or develop research datasets. The proposed contributions and opportunities are interpretative suggestions, not confirmed institutional commitments or a verified inventory of available datasets.

Interpretation and feedback. Automated classifications, definitions and familiarity assessments are exploratory and may contain omissions or errors. They should not be treated as independently validated findings. Readers can flag unfamiliar or ambiguous expressions from the Abstracts view; submissions are intended for review and possible refinement of the terminology collection.

Cross-cutting methodological skills

Laboratory science, genomics and immunology

Sequencing, pathogen biology, antibody profiling and other molecular approaches connect mechanistic research with disease surveillance and vaccine evaluation.

Epidemiology, statistics, modelling and AI

Clinical trials, observational studies, spatial and mathematical modelling, machine learning and computational methods appear across disease areas.

Implementation, policy and social science

Qualitative and mixed-methods work, community engagement, service evaluation and policy translation link research findings to delivery contexts.

What the portfolio suggests

Infectious diseases dominate the meeting's submissions. The malaria and TB, vaccines, and pathogenesis/transmission sections together account for 27 abstracts, or approximately 55%.

Research themes are interconnected. Antimicrobial resistance, transmission, vaccine effects and modelling span several handbook sections; a single-label classification conceals those links.

Computational approaches are cross-cutting rather than a dominant standalone section. Machine learning, geospatial analysis and modelling are present, but the abstract collection alone cannot establish the size of an institutional data science programme.

Scope and limitations

This is a descriptive and interpretative analysis of conference abstracts, not an institutional ranking. Abstract counts do not establish funding, staffing, publication impact or long-term programme strength. Stream descriptions and methodological assessments are inferred from the abstracts, whereas the nine section counts are taken directly from the handbook metadata.

2. Computational simulation in the ASM abstracts

Under this definition, computational simulation requires a dynamic or generative process where you change variables to see how a system behaves over time or under hypothetical conditions (e.g., Monte Carlo simulations, agent-based modeling, or climate models). The use of statistical regression, machine-learning prediction, mathematical estimation and computational data processing alone without a computational simulation do not meet this criterion.

Interpretation. The broader figure describes the prevalence of computational simulation within the reported research. The stricter figure provides a more conservative indication of direct simulation activity. A16 reports the use of a calibrated transmission model's outputs, while A30 relies on climate-model simulations; their abstracts do not establish whether the study teams developed the underlying simulation models. The stricter classification should not be read as proof of original model development or ownership.

Limitations. These classifications reflect what is explicitly described in the 49 abstracts, not a code audit or a complete inventory of MLW modelling activity. Studies may contain unreported simulations, and direct model execution does not necessarily mean the authors created the model. The estimates are descriptive proportions, not institutional capability metrics.
