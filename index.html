MLW Research Terminology Explorer

This is an interface to a dictionary of terminology extracted from abstracts presented at the MLW ASM in Mangochi, 4–7 October 2026. (@DSCM Unit)

It explores specialist language that may require explanation across disciplinary boundaries.

"Methods in short"

The identification of research domains and classification of abstracts into these domains are GPT-driven. Technical term extraction and the assessment of whether terms may be familiar or unfamiliar to researchers from different disciplines are also automated.

A technical term is defined here as a word or multi-word expression whose interpretation depends substantially on specialised disciplinary, methodological, scientific or professional knowledge.

GPT extracts these terms without an explicitly defined or reproducible set of rules. The model does not appear to rely on established terminology lexicons; instead, extraction likely reflects contextual usage and semantic specificity—the extent to which a term conveys a specialised meaning within a particular domain. Although this limits standardisation and reproducibility, it also offers opportunities to identify terminology that conventional lexicons may overlook.

Linguistic specificity is a related but distinct concept. It concerns whether a noun phrase refers to a particular, identifiable entity in context, rather than whether the expression carries specialised disciplinary meaning.

To complement this automated process, the Abstracts dashboard allows users to identify and submit terms that may be unfamiliar within their own disciplines. Submissions are recorded in a Google spreadsheet for review and potential inclusion in future versions of the lexicon. This provides a simple, transparent mechanism for incorporating disciplinary feedback.

Cross-cutting methodological capabilities

Laboratory science, genomics and immunology

Sequencing, pathogen biology, antibody profiling and other molecular approaches connect mechanistic research with disease surveillance and vaccine evaluation.

Epidemiology, statistics, modelling and AI

Clinical trials, observational studies, spatial and mathematical modelling, machine learning and computational methods appear across disease areas.

Implementation, policy and social science

Qualitative and mixed-methods work, community engagement, service evaluation and policy translation link research findings to delivery contexts.

What the portfolio suggests

Infectious diseases dominate the meeting's submissions. The malaria and TB, vaccines, and pathogenesis/transmission sections together account for 27 abstracts, or approximately 55%.

Research themes are interconnected. Antimicrobial resistance, transmission, vaccine effects and modelling span several handbook sections; a single-label classification conceals those links.

Computational approaches are cross-cutting rather than a dominant standalone section. Machine learning, geospatial analysis and modelling are present, but the abstract collection alone cannot establish the size of an institutional data science programme.

Scope and limitations

This is a descriptive and interpretative analysis of conference abstracts, not an institutional ranking. Abstract counts do not establish funding, staffing, publication impact or long-term programme strength. Stream descriptions and methodological assessments are inferred from the abstracts, whereas the nine section counts are taken directly from the handbook metadata.

Confirming the baseline

Research-group discussions should confirm local expertise, contributor roles, dataset custodians, access conditions, software availability and collaborator time before resources are prioritised. This page is a preliminary assessment of abstracts, not a completed institutional capability audit.
